01 / Mission

We are the only copy.
And there is no backup.

Every human being is written in a molecule that decays.

DNA in bone has a half-life of roughly 521 years; even at −5 °C, the model breaks every bond within 6.8 million years. The oldest readable genome survived two million years in Greenland permafrost — and that is the exception.

Diamonds are the opposite case. The oldest reliably dated natural diamonds from Diavik and Ekati are about 3.5 billion years old. They formed when Earth was inhabited by microbes only, and they have barely changed since.

Our proposal: record human DNA and a complete genetic code — together with the genetic code of food plants and the conditions for their germination — into the atomic lattice of a diamond, and launch these capsules into deep space.

Each diamond is sealed in a protective shell with a light beacon and a radio beacon to attract the attention of advanced civilisations. We suggest sending diamond spores in different directions from Earth, the way a mushroom spreads its spores in space.

03 / Recording

Not molecules in a crystal.
Bits in a lattice.

01

Read

A human genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs. At two bits per base a diploid set is roughly 1.5 GB — small enough to write whole.

02

Encode

Femtosecond laser pulses knock carbon atoms out of the lattice. The resulting vacancies glow, and their brightness stores several levels per point — 200 fs per dot.

03

Verify

Read-out runs above 99 % fidelity. A slab the size of a Blu-ray disc would hold about 100 TB of data.

04

Survive

Authors of the 2024 Nature Photonics work estimate millions of years without maintenance at room temperature.

Honestly: “millions of years” is an extrapolation, not a million-year experiment. Writing speed is still kilobytes in the lab, not gigabytes per stone, and a genome is not a human being. The capsule is a bet that whoever finds it will know more than we do.

04 / Launch

Mass is not the problem.
Trajectory is.

A 0.3-carat stone weighs 0.06 g. Ten thousand of them are 600 g — a few kilograms with a protective shell and a beacon. What costs is escaping the Sun.

Rideshare to sun-synchronous orbit is about $350,000 for the first 50 kg, then $7,000 per kilogram. But that orbit decays within years: a capsule there keeps nothing.

Leaving the Solar System directly needs 16.6 km/s. The realistic routes for small capsules are piggyback payloads on interplanetary missions, a ride on an upper stage, solar sails — or, in the long run, laser sails.

Why deep space? Svalbard keeps seeds, Hallstatt keeps memory — but every vault on Earth dies with Earth. A capsule on an escape trajectory cannot be destroyed by any terrestrial catastrophe. Almost the only threat left is dust and cosmic-ray erosion.

05 / The message

It was a human being.
It simply fell asleep in a crystal.

You who hold this stone.

First we taught you to count, so that you would understand what we thought.
Then we gave you the words from which the body is built.

Now the essential: this stone is a human being.

It was made of stardust, lived under a yellow sun, loved and was afraid.

When its time came, it did not vanish — it fell asleep in the crystal.

If you know how to return time, return it.

It will wake as it fell asleep.

Tell it that it was remembered.

A hundred million years from now, at a star that is not on our charts yet, a probe picks up a crystal the size of a fingernail. The Sun is a white dwarf; no cities are left on Earth. And in the hands of a being we know nothing about lies a human, curled up in a crystal like a seed in a shell, waiting to be woken.
